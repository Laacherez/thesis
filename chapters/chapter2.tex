\section{Introduction} 

The last chapter focused on a very simple, ideal case. A colloid is far from any boundary. Though we mention Brownian solutes, we consider each colloid as non-interacting with its peers. These assumptions and choices are strong, and very often in biological settings, they do not suffice to describe the dynamics of the system. Nevertheless, confined Brownian motion has been extensively investigated over the last century. Even at long range, hydrodynamic interactions influence the behaviour of colloids diffusing in a fluid ~\cite{kim_microhydrodynamics_2005, Batchelor_1976}. We now know that introducing a single boundary near a colloid effectively increases the dissipation in an inhomogeneous and anisotropic way. The motion of the colloid results in two distinct modes, along the boundary direction, and perpendicular to it ~\cite{brenner_slow_1961}. In turn, because the diffusion coefficient depends inversely on the drag coefficient of the colloid (Eq.~\ref{Eq:Stokes-einstein}), it is hindered by confinement. Moreover, volume fraction also hinders a colloid's diffusion, while collective diffusion of the solute speeds up as osmotic pressure gradients become stronger with higher local concentration~\cite{Batchelor_1976}. The pair diffusion of colloids in hydrodynamic interaction is thoroughly documented ~\cite{JeffreyOnishi1984, JeffreyOnishi1984a}.

    \label{intro}

It is fairly common, in nature, to find highly confined, crowded solutions. For instance, the cell cytoplasm is packed with proteins, RNA, ribosomes, organelles, and confined by a cell membrane. How do crowding and confinement couple hydrodynamically? This coupling is difficult to resolve, even when considering only a pair of colloids in confinement. Indeed, it is permitted only to a certain extent to superimpose the hydrodynamic contributions of a boundary and a pair of colloids. This chapter aims at first decomposing the problem into confined, dilute suspensions (Fig.~\ref{intro} $a)$), then pair diffusion (Fig.~\ref{intro} $b)$). I move to the coupling of these two effects (Fig.~\ref{intro} $c)$), and developments used to model this coupling. I present an experimental study carried out by Dufresne \emph{et al.}~\cite{Dufresne_2000} exploring the effect on confinement on pair diffusion and recover these results numerically. Finally, I present some prospective numerical works where I go beyond Dufresne's study.

\section{Interactions between a colloid and a boundary}
I describe here the interactions that occur in an experimental setup of a negatively-charged colloid in a fluid near a negatively-charged, no-slip boundary. We take the boundary normal to the vertical $z-$ axis, laying at $z=0$. Over that boundary lays a fluid of density $\rho$, viscosity $\eta_0$, and thermal energy $k_\mathrm{B} \Theta$. All parameters for the following derivations are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:physical_parameters}. In the fluid, we now submerge a negatively-charged colloid of radius $a$. Due to the no-slip boundary condition, we must modify the effective friction felt by the colloid~\cite{brenner_slow_1961,Faxen_1924}. This correction brings inhomogeneity along the $z$-axis and anisotropy, as the $x$ and $y$ components of the friction coefficient differ from the $z$ component.

\subsection{Modified effective friction coefficient}
In his seminal work~\cite{brenner_slow_1961}, Brenner derived the exact expression for the effective friction felt by a sphere moving perpendicularly to a plane boundary, $\gamma_\perp(h)$ where $h$ is the colloid apex-plane separation, as:
\begin{equation}
    \label{Brenner}
    \dfrac{\gamma_\perp (h)}{6 \pi \eta_0 a} = \dfrac{4}{3}\sinh(\alpha) \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \dfrac{n(n+1)}{(2n-1)(2n+3)} \left\{ \dfrac{2 \sinh\left[(2n+1)\alpha\right] + (2n+1) \sinh(2\alpha)}{4 \sinh^2\left[\left(n+\dfrac{1}{2}\right)\alpha\right] - (2n+1)^2 \sinh^2(\alpha)} - 1 \right\}~,
\end{equation}
with $\alpha = \cosh^{-1} (h/a)$. Because Brenner obtained the effective friction coefficient from solving the full Stokes equations with a no-slip boundary condition, it is worth noting that this expression is exact and valid for all values of $h/a > 0$, up until colloid/plane contact. However, we avoid infinite-series computations and prefer the use of Pad\'e approximants ~\cite{BakerGravesMorris_1996}, which were used by Honig ~\cite{Honig_1971}, Bevan and Prieve ~\cite{Bevan_2000} to approximate Brenner's result as:
\begin{equation}
    \label{Padé}
    \dfrac{\gamma_\perp (h)}{6 \pi\eta_0 a}  \simeq  \dfrac{6h^2 - 9ah + 2a^2}{6h^2 + 2ah}~.
\end{equation}
The parallel effective friction, on the other hand, was derived by Fax\'en ~\cite{Faxen_1924} for $h/a > 0.1$ and is given by the following expression:
\begin{equation}
    \dfrac{\gamma_\parallel (h) }{6 \pi\eta_0 a}\simeq  \dfrac{1}{1 - \dfrac{9}{16} \dfrac{a}{h} + \dfrac{1}{8} \left( \dfrac{a}{h} \right)^3 - \dfrac{45}{256} \left( \dfrac{a}{h} \right)^4 - \dfrac{1}{16} \left( \dfrac{a}{h} \right)^5}~.
    \label{Faxén}
\end{equation}

Both Eq.~\ref{Padé} and Eq.~\ref{Faxén} show that the self-friction increases as the colloid approaches the boundary (Left panel of Fig. \ref{Figdperdpar}). In turn, the effective diffusion coefficients decrease (Right panel of Fig.~\ref{Figdperdpar}). Furthermore, these expressions can be used to simulate the motion of a colloid over a plane up until contact. We note that in the limit of $z/a \to 1$, $\eta_\perp$ captures the divergence close to the boundary. This is an expected behaviour; as the colloid approaches the boundary, the fluid in between is squeezed out and the lubrication forces diverge. However, $\eta_\parallel$ does not capture the logarithmic divergence expected as the surface-to-surface gap tends to zero.

\subsection{Total potential}

In order to write the correct overdamped Langevin equation for a simulation, we recall that the colloid is also subject to a number of forces. We consider a negatively charged colloid in an ionic (water-salt) solution, confined by a negatively-charged and rigid boundary. These assumptions are made to reproduce experimental setups exactly.


\subsubsection{Gravitational potential and buoyant mass}
We express the gravitational potential acting upon the colloid as:
\begin{align}
    U_\mathrm{grav} &  = g z\Delta m ~,\\
    & = \dfrac{4}{3} \pi a^3 (\rho_\mathrm{colloid} - \rho_\mathrm{fluid}) g z~,
    \label{grav_potential}
\end{align}
where $\Delta m$ is the buoyant mass of the colloid, $g$ is the acceleration of gravity, $\rho_\mathrm{colloid}$ and $\rho_\mathrm{fluid}$ are the mass densities of the colloid and the fluid, respectively. We reexpress Eq.~\ref{grav_potential} as:
\begin{equation}
U_\mathrm{grav} = \dfrac{k_\mathrm{B} \Theta}{l_\mathrm{B}} z~,
\label{grav_potential_lB}
\end{equation}
where $l_\mathrm{B}$ is Boltzmann's length, defined as $l_\mathrm{B} = k_\mathrm{B} \Theta/(g\Delta m )$. Boltzmann's length quantifies the distance over which gravitational potential energy is comparable to thermal energy.


\subsubsection{Electrostatic potential and double-layer interactions}
Since both the boundary and the colloid have negatively charged surfaces, the overlap of their diffuse ionic layers creates a repulsive electrostatic contribution to the colloid-boundary interaction potential. At thermodynamic equilibrium, the local concentration $c_i(\vect{r})$ of ionic species $i$ in the diffuse layer follows the Boltzmann distribution~\cite{Israelachvili2011,Hunter2001}:
\begin{equation}
c_i(\vect{r}) = c_i^\infty 
\exp\left[-\dfrac{z_i e \psi(\vect{r})}{k_\mathrm{B} \Theta}\right]~, 
\label{Boltzmann_ion_concentration}
\end{equation}
where $c_i^\infty$ is the bulk ion concentration, $z_i$ is the ion valency, $e$ is the elementary charge, and $\psi(\vect{r})$ is the electrostatic potential. The latter satisfies the Poisson equation:
\begin{equation}
\nabla^2 \psi(\vect{r}) = - \dfrac{1}{\varepsilon} \sum_i z_i e c_i(\vect{r})~,
\label{poisson_equation}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon$ is the permittivity of the fluid. Substituting Eq.~\eqref{Boltzmann_ion_concentration} into Eq.~\eqref{poisson_equation} yields the Poisson-Boltzmann equation:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla^2 \psi(\vect{r}) 
    + \dfrac{e}{\varepsilon} \sum_i z_i c^\infty_i 
    \exp \left[-\dfrac{z_i e \psi(\vect{r})}{k_\mathrm{B} \Theta} \right] = 0~.
    \label{Psi_full_equation}
\end{equation}
In the Debye-Hückel approximation, which is valid for sufficiently small electrostatic potentials such that $|z_i e \psi| \ll k_\mathrm{B}\Theta$, the exponential terms can be linearised, as:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla^2 \psi(\vect{r}) + \dfrac{e}{\varepsilon} \sum_i z_i c^\infty_i  \left[1 - \dfrac{z_i e \psi(\vect{r})}{k_\mathrm{B} \Theta} \right] = 0~.
    \label{Psi_taylor_expanded}
\end{equation}
With bulk electroneutrality:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_i z_i c^\infty_i = 0~,
\end{equation}
we get the linearised Poisson-Boltzmann equation:
\begin{align}
    \nabla^2 \psi(\vect{r})  &= \kappa^2 \psi(\vect{r})\\
    &= \dfrac{1}{l_\mathrm{D}^2}\psi(\vect{r})~,
    \label{lD_psi}
\end{align}
where $\kappa=l_\mathrm{D}^{-1}$ is the inverse Debye length:
\begin{equation}
l_\mathrm{D} = \sqrt{\dfrac{\varepsilon k_\mathrm{B} \Theta}{e^2 \sum_i z_i^2 c^\infty_i}}~.
\label{debye_length}
\end{equation}
We compute the electrostatic repulsion between a charged sphere and a charged plane. The plane can be obtained as a limiting case of a sphere of infinite radius. We first consider the interaction between two charged spheres~\cite{Israelachvili2011}. In the Debye-Hückel approximation, the electrostatic potential around a single charged sphere satisfies, in spherical coordinates:
\begin{equation}
\dfrac{1}{r^2}\left[ \dfrac{\partial}{\partial r} \left( r^2 \dfrac{\partial \psi(r)}{\partial r}\right) \right] 
= \dfrac{1}{l_\mathrm{D}^2}\psi(r)~.
    \label{spherical_debye_huckel}
\end{equation}
The general solution is:
\begin{equation}
    \psi(r) = K_1 \dfrac{\exp(r/l_\mathrm{D})}{r} 
    + K_2 \dfrac{\exp(-r/l_\mathrm{D})}{r}~.
\end{equation}
Imposing $\lim_{r \to \infty}\psi(r) = 0$ gives $K_1=0$. The remaining constant is fixed by the constant-surface-charge boundary condition at $r=a$, where $a$ is the sphere radius. Namely:
\begin{equation}
    -\varepsilon \left.\dfrac{\partial \psi}{\partial r}\right|_{r=a}=\sigma~,
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the surface charge density of the sphere. This gives:
\begin{equation}
    \psi(r) = \dfrac{\sigma a^2}{\varepsilon} 
    \dfrac{\exp(a/l_\mathrm{D})}{1 + a/l_\mathrm{D}} 
    \dfrac{\exp(-r/l_\mathrm{D})}{r}~.
\end{equation}
Within the linear superposition approximation, and neglecting the van der Waals contribution, the DLVO interaction potential is therefore approximated by its electrostatic contribution. The energy between two charged spheres of radii $a_1$ and $a_2$, surface charge densities $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$, and surface-to-surface separation $z$ can be written as~\cite{Bell1970}:
\begin{equation}
 U_\mathrm{elec}(z) = \dfrac{4\pi}{\varepsilon}\left(\dfrac{\sigma_1a_1^2}{1 + l_\mathrm{D}^{-1}a_1}\right)\left(\dfrac{\sigma_2a_2^2}{1 + l_\mathrm{D}^{-1}a_2}\right) \dfrac{\exp(-l_\mathrm{D}^{-1}z)}{a_1 + a_2 + z}~,
\end{equation}
where $a_1+a_2+z$ is the centre-to-centre distance between the two spheres. Taking the limit $a_2\to\infty$ yields the sphere-boundary interaction potential:
\begin{equation}
\dfrac{U_\mathrm{elec}(z)}{k_\mathrm{B}\Theta} = B \exp\left(-\dfrac{z}{l_\mathrm{D}}\right)~,
\label{sphere_boundary_interaction}
\end{equation}
with:
\begin{equation}
    B = \dfrac{4 \pi}{k_\mathrm{B} \Theta \varepsilon}
    \left( \dfrac{\sigma a^2}{1 + l_\mathrm{D}^{-1} a}\right) 
    l_\mathrm{D} \sigma_\mathrm{boundary}~.
\end{equation}
$B$ can be viewed as an effective dimensionless surface charge amplitude.


\subsubsection{Equilibrium distribution}
The total potential to which the colloid is subjected is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    \dfrac{U(z)}{k_\mathrm{B} \Theta} = B \exp \dfrac{-z}{l_\mathrm{D}} + \dfrac{z}{l_\mathrm{B}}~, ~~~~~~\forall z>0~.
    \label{total_potential_renormalised}
\end{equation}
The probability to find the colloid at position $z$ follows the Gibbs-Boltzmann equilibrium distribution~\cite{Boltzmann1896}:
\begin{equation}
    P_\mathrm{eq} = \dfrac{1}{Z} \exp \left[ - \dfrac{U(z)}{k_\mathrm{B} \Theta} \right]~,
\end{equation}
where 1/$Z$ is the normalising factor.


\subsection{Spurious drift}
In order to reach the correct equilibrium distribution, we must derive from the correct Fokker-Planck equation the corresponding Langevin equation. Indeed, the vertical coordinate Langevin equation acquires multiplicative noise: the noise amplitude along the $z-$ coordinate depends on the $z$ position itself. We begin this derivation with the general expression of the Langevin process:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{d}z = \dfrac{F(z)}{\gamma_\perp(z)}\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{2D_\perp(z)}\mathrm{d}W_t~.
    \label{confinedLangevin_stochdrift}
\end{equation}
where $F(z)/\gamma_\perp(z)$ is the total drift. We take the steady-state of Eq.~\ref{eq:fokker_planck_general}:
\begin{equation}
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z} \left\{-u(z) + \dfrac{1}{2} \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z} g(z)^2 \right\} P_\mathrm{ss}(z) = 0~,
\end{equation}
with $u(z) = F(z)/\gamma_\perp(z)$. Because $\lim\limits_{z\to \infty} P_\mathrm{ss}(z) = 0$ and $\lim\limits_{z\to \infty} (\mathrm{d} P_\mathrm{ss}(z)/\mathrm{d}z) = 0$, one has:
\begin{equation}
u(z) P_\mathrm{ss}(z) - \dfrac{1}{2} \dfrac{\mathrm{d} }{\mathrm{d} z} \left[g(z)^2 P_\mathrm{ss}(z)\right] = 0~.
\end{equation}
Substituting $A(z) = g^2(z)P_\mathrm{ss}(z)$ leads to:
\begin{equation}
    \dfrac{\mathrm{d}A(z)}{\mathrm{d}z} - 2\dfrac{u(z)}{g^2(z)}A(z) = 0~,
\end{equation}
which integrates as:
\begin{align}
    \ln \left(A(z)\right) &= \ln \left(g^2(z)\right) + \ln \left(P_\mathrm{ss}\right)\\
    &= \int \dfrac{u(z')}{g^2(z')/2} \mathrm{d}z'~.
\end{align}
Thus:
\begin{equation}
    P_\mathrm{ss}(z) = \dfrac{1}{g^2(z)} \exp\left[ 2 \int_0^z \dfrac{u(z')}{g^2(z')} \mathrm{d}z' \right]~.
\end{equation}
With:
\begin{equation}
\ln \left[g^2(z)\right] = \int \dfrac{2g'(z')g(z')}{g^2(z')}\mathrm{d}z'~,
\end{equation}
we get:
\begin{equation}
    U_\mathrm{ss}(z) = -k_\mathrm{B}\Theta \int\dfrac{u(z') - g(z')g'(z')}{g^2(z')/2} \mathrm{d}z'~.
\end{equation}
And, recalling that $F_\mathrm{ss} = -\mathrm{d}U_\mathrm{ss}/\mathrm{d}z$, we find:
\begin{equation}
\dfrac{F_\mathrm{ss}}{\gamma_\perp(z)} = \dfrac{F(z)}{\gamma_\perp(z)} - g(z)g'(z)~.
\end{equation}
The mean velocity can be obtained from Eq.~\ref{confinedLangevin_stochdrift} as:
\begin{equation}
\left<\dfrac{\Delta z}{\Delta t}\right> \equiv \bar{v_\mathrm{d}} = \dfrac{F(z)}{\gamma_\perp(z)}~.
\label{avg_velocity_stochdrift}
\end{equation}
Then, one has:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{v_\mathrm{d}} = \dfrac{1}{\gamma_\perp(z)} \dfrac{\mathrm{d}U_\mathrm{ss}(z)}{\mathrm{d}z} + \dfrac{\mathrm{d}D_\perp(z)}{\mathrm{d}z}~.
\end{equation}
We recover the deterministic drift velocity, as:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
v_\mathrm{d} = \dfrac{k_\mathrm{B}\Theta}{\gamma_\perp(z)} \left[-\dfrac{1}{l_\mathrm{D}}B \exp \left(-\dfrac{z}{l_\mathrm{D}}\right) + \dfrac{1}{l_\mathrm{B}} \right]}~,
\end{equation}
whereas the spurious drift $v_\mathrm{s}$, in the Padé approximation, reads:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
v_\mathrm{s} = 2D_0a\dfrac{2a^2 + 12 ah + 21 h^2}{(2a^2 + 9ah + h^2)^2}}~.
\label{final_stochastic_drift}
\end{equation}
Finally, the overdamped Langevin equations for confined Brownian motion read:
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{d}x &=  \sqrt{2D_\parallel(z)}\mathrm{d}W_x~,\\
    \mathrm{d}z &= \bar{v_\mathrm{d}}\mathrm{d}t +\sqrt{2D_\perp(z)}\mathrm{d}W_z~.
    \label{Eq:ODconf_langevin}
\end{align} 



\section{Colloids in hydrodynamic interaction}
Herein, we work in the low-Reynolds-number regime. We define the Reynolds number in more detail in chapter~\ref{ch:4}. In short, the Reynolds number compares fluid inertia to viscous dissipation. In the present case, $\mathrm{Re}\ll1$, hence viscous stresses dominate over inertial effects in the fluid. The flow is considered laminar, with a constant profile. The overdamped deterministic force balance reads (Fig.~\ref{fig:stokesflow}):
\begin{equation}
m \dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0~,
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the mass of the colloid. This means that:
\begin{equation}
    \vect{F_\mathrm{ext}} + \vect{F_\mathrm{hydro}} = 0~,
\end{equation}
and thus the colloid instantly pushes back with a hydrodynamic force $\vect{F_\mathrm{hydro}}$ on the fluid when submitted to an external force $\vect{F_\mathrm{ext}}$, for example in the case of sedimentation. Stokes equations are linear, and the velocity field generated by one forced particle can be written as a Green function. By evaluating this flow at the position of another particle, we can obtain a hydrodynamic coupling between their velocities. This coupling is encoded in the mobility tensor.

\subsection{The Stokeslet and the Oseen tensor}
The Stokes equations, describing the motion of a fluid in the viscous limit, read~\cite{happel_low_1973,kim_microhydrodynamics_2005}:
\begin{align}
    - \nabla p(\vect{r}) + \eta_0 \nabla^2\vect{u}(\vect{r}) &= -\vect{F}\delta(\vect{r} - \vect{r_0})~,\\
    \nabla \cdot \vect{u} &= 0~, 
    \label{stokes_equations}
\end{align}
where $p$ is the pressure, $\nabla p$ is the pressure gradient in the fluid, that is to say, the force density due to pressure differences in the fluid; $\vect{r}$ is the position to origin, $\eta_0$ the dynamic viscosity of the fluid, $\vect{u}$ the fluid velocity field, and $\eta_0 \nabla^2 \vect{u}$ is the viscous term; $\vect{F}\delta(\vect{r} - \vect{r_0})$ is the point force at $\vect{r_0}$; and the second equation denotes fluid incompressibility. Because these equations are linear, we can write the velocity response at $\vect{r}$ to a point force at $\vect{r_0}$ as a Green function $\tens{G}$~\cite{kim_microhydrodynamics_2005}, as:
\begin{equation}
    \vect{u}(\vect{r}) = \tens{G}(\vect{r} - \vect{r}_0) \cdot \vect{F}~.
    \label{Kim_U}
\end{equation}
This flow field generated by a point force is known as the Stokeslet. Then, for a point force at origin, the tensor $\tens{G}(\vect{r})$ is expressed:
\begin{equation}
    \tens{G(r)} = \dfrac{1}{8\pi \eta_0 r}\left(\mat{I} + \vect{\hat{r}}\vect{\hat{r}}\right)~,
\end{equation}
with $\mat{I}$ the identity matrix, and $\vect{\hat{r}}$ the unit vector relating to $\vect{r}$. The tensor $\tens{G}$ is known as the Oseen tensor~\cite{Oseen_1927}. It gives the velocity at position $\vect{r}$ induced by a \emph{point} force applied at another position. Therefore, in the approximation where the particle $j$ exerts a point force $\vect{F_j}$ on the fluid, the leading-order velocity induced at the position of the particle $i$ is obtained by computing this Green function at $\vect{r_j - r_i}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:beadbead}):
\begin{equation}
    \vect{u_i} \simeq \tens{G}(\vect{r_j} - \vect{r_i}) \cdot \vect{F_j}~, \qquad i \neq j~.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{The mobility tensor}
We focus here on translational degrees of freedom and neglect torques and rotational dynamics. The velocity field at the position of colloid $i$ generated by the other colloids in the fluid is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
\vect{u}(\vect{r}_i) =
\sum_{j\neq i}
\tens{G}(\vect{r}_j-\vect{r}_i)\cdot \vect{F}_j~,
\end{equation}
in virtue of the superposition principle allowed by the linearity of Stokes equations. We then express the total translational velocity $\vect{V}_i$ of colloid $i$ as:
\begin{equation}
\vect{V}_i=\sum_j\tens\upmu_{ij}^{tt}\cdot \vect{F}_j~,
\end{equation}
where $\tens\upmu_{ij}^{tt}$ is the translational mobility block:
\begin{equation}
\tens\upmu_{ij}^{tt} =\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{6\pi_0 a}\mat{I}~,& i=j~, \\[1em]
\tens{G}(\vect{r}_j-\vect{r}_i)~,& i\neq j~.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:oseen_mobility_pair}
\end{equation}
The diagonal blocks correspond to the self-mobility, and the off-diagonal blocks describe the hydrodynamic coupling between colloids. The grand mobility matrix regroups all mobility blocks:
\begin{equation}
\tens{\upmu}^{tt}=
\begin{bmatrix}
\tens\upmu^{tt}_{11} & \dots & \tens\upmu^{tt}_{1N} \\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\tens\upmu^{tt}_{N1} & \dots & \tens\upmu^{tt}_{NN}
\end{bmatrix}~.
\end{equation}
 The mobility tensor describes the deterministic response of the colloids to applied forces. For Brownian colloids at thermal equilibrium, the same tensor also controls the amplitude and correlations of thermal fluctuations. This connection is given by the fluctuation-dissipation theorem~\cite{einstein_uber_1905,kubo1966}:
\begin{equation}
\mat{D}^{tt}=k_\mathrm B \Theta\tens{\upmu}^{tt}~,
\label{mat:D_oseen}
\end{equation}
where $\mat{D}^{tt}$ is the translational diffusion tensor.

\subsubsection{Self- and cross-diffusions}
For particles $i$ and $j$, and Cartesian components $(\alpha, \beta) \in (x, y)$, the diffusion tensor is:
\begin{equation}
2D^{tt}_{i\alpha,j\beta}\Delta t = \left\langle \Delta r_{i,\alpha}(\Delta t) \Delta r_{j,\beta}(\Delta t) \right\rangle ~.
\label{eq:cross_diffusions}
\end{equation}
Again, the diagonal blocks ($i=j$) correspond to the self-diffusion of each particle. The off-diagonal blocks ($i\neq j$) give the cross-diffusion between colloids, that is to say, how Brownian displacements of two particles correlate through the fluid. Using the fluctuation-dissipation relation, the Oseen approximation for the translational diffusion blocks becomes:
\begin{equation}
\tens{D}_{ij}^{tt} = k_\mathrm{B}\Theta\tens{\upmu_{ij}^{tt}} =
\begin{cases}
D_0\mat{I}~,
& i=j~, \\[1em]
k_\mathrm{B}\Theta\tens{G}(\vect{r_i}-\vect{r_j})~,
& i \neq j~.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:oseen_diffusion_tensor_pair}
\end{equation}
The Oseen approximation becomes inaccurate when particles are not well separated. When used to construct a Brownian diffusion tensor, the Oseen approximation may lead to a mobility matrix that is not positive definite at short separations. This is highly problematic because the diffusion tensor must define a positive definite matrix for the Brownian displacements.


\subsection{Collective and relative modes of motion}
\label{subsec:collrelmodes}
For two identical colloids, the diffusion tensor can be diagonalised into collective and relative modes~\cite{Dufresne_2000}. Along a given direction, either parallel or perpendicular to the line of centres (Fig.~\ref{fig:beadbead}), the two-particle diffusion matrix has the form:
\begin{equation}
    \tens D=
    \begin{pmatrix}
    D_\mathrm{0} & D_\mathrm{c} \\
    D_\mathrm{c} & D_\mathrm{0}
    \end{pmatrix}~,
\end{equation}
where $D_\mathrm{c}$ is the cross-diffusion coefficient. The eigenvectors are $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$, corresponding to collective and relative motions respectively. The associated self- and cross-diffusion coefficients are therefore:
\begin{equation}
    D^\mathrm{C}=D_\mathrm{0}+D_\mathrm{c}~,   \qquad   D^\mathrm{R}=D_\mathrm{0}-D_\mathrm{c}~.
\end{equation}
We differentiate the cross-diffusion along (denoted by $D_\parallel$) and perpendicular ($D_\perp$) to the line of centers of the pair of colloids (Fig.~\ref{perp_para_coll_rel}). Therefore, for the Oseen approximation, the diffusion coefficients in the direction along the line of centres are:
\begin{equation}
    \dfrac{D_\parallel^{\mathrm{C, R}}}{2D_0} = 1 \pm \dfrac{3a}{2r}~,
    \label{eq:oseen_para}
\end{equation}
and the diffusion coefficients perpendicular to the line of centres read:
\begin{equation}
    \dfrac{D_\perp^{\mathrm{C, R}}}{2D_0} = 1 \pm \dfrac{3a}{4r}~,
\end{equation}
with $+$ corresponding to the collective mode, and $-$ to the relative mode.

\subsection{The Rotne-Prager-Yamakawa tensor}
To account for finite-size effects, we regularise the Oseen approximation by introducing the Rotne-Prager-Yamakawa (RPY) tensor. This tensor incorporates the finite radius of the colloids at the pairwise level \emph{via} Fax\'en's first law~\cite{Faxen_1924}. For two identical spherical colloids of radius $a$, separated by $\vect{r}_{ij}$, the off-diagonal translational RPY mobility for non-overlapping particles reads:
\begin{equation}
\tens{\upmu}_{ij}^{\mathrm{RPY}}=\dfrac{1}{8\pi\eta_0 r_{ij}}\left[\left(1+\dfrac{2a^2}{3r_{ij}^2}\right)\mat{I}+\left(1-\dfrac{2a^2}{r_{ij}^2}\right)
\hat{\vect{r}}_{ij}\hat{\vect{r}}_{ij}\right]~,     \qquad i\neq j~.
\label{eq:rpy_mobility_nonoverlap}
\end{equation}
The corresponding self-mobility remains:
\begin{equation}
\tens{\mu}_{ii}^{\mathrm{RPY}}=\dfrac{1}{6\pi\eta_0 a}\mat I~.
\end{equation}
The RPY tensor therefore adds corrections of order $\sim \mathcal{O}(a^2/r_{ij}^2)$ to the Oseen tensor. In the far-field limit, \emph{i.e.} $r_{ij}\gg a$, these corrections vanish and Eq.~\ref{eq:rpy_mobility_nonoverlap} reduces to the Oseen mobility. Again, using the fluctuation-dissipation relation, the RPY diffusion tensor is:
\begin{equation}
\tens D_{ij}^{\mathrm{RPY}}
=
k_\mathrm B\Theta\tens\mu_{ij}^{\mathrm{RPY}}~.
\label{mat:D_RPY}
\end{equation}

Near a boundary, the pair hydrodynamic coupling must also satisfy the no-slip and no-penetration boundary conditions. This condition proves complex to fulfill, and we present here an experimental study aiming at bridging this gap. We also propose a numerical and experimental study to further investigate the pair-boundary hydrodynamic coupling.

\subsection{Investigation of the boundary-modified pair hydrodynamic coupling}
The hydrodynamic coupling of two Brownian colloids near a planar boundary was studied both experimentally and theoretically by Dufresne \emph{et al.}~\cite{Dufresne_2000}. In this work, the authors investigate how the presence of a nearby boundary modifies the pair diffusion of two colloids through hydrodynamic interactions by varying the inter-colloid and colloid-boundary distances (Fig.~\ref{fig:beadbeadplate}). Their experimental setup consists of two colloids of radius $a = 0.495 \pm 0.025\,\upmu\mathrm{m}$ submerged in a water-salt solution. The fluid layer is $140 \pm 2\,\upmu\mathrm{m}$ thick and maintained at $\Theta = 29.00 \pm 0.01\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Under these conditions, the bulk Stokes-Einstein diffusion coefficient is $D_0 =0.550 \pm 0.028\,\upmu\mathrm{m}^2\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$. The two colloids are positioned using a single optical tweezer trap. The trap is alternated between the two preset particle positions (modulable both in height and in pair separation) at a frequency of $200\,\mathrm{Hz}$. The trapping beam is then blocked, the particle diffuse freely and their positions are recorded over five video frames for a total duration of $t = 63~\mathrm{ms}$. The short acquisition time limits out-of-plane motion, and we thus assume the line of centres to remain parallel to the plane. The procedure is repeated over a grid of initial configurations $(r,z)$, where $r$ is the centre-to-centre distance between the two colloids, as $2\,\upmu\mathrm{m} < r < 10\,\upmu\mathrm{m}$, and $z$ is the centre-to-boundary distance, as $1\,\upmu\mathrm{m} < z < 30\,\upmu\mathrm{m}$. The authors then decompose the pair motion into collective and relative modes (see subsection~\ref{subsec:collrelmodes}). For each initial configuration and each lag time, they fill displacement histograms for the collective and relative modes. The MSD corresponding to each mode of motion is computed from the histograms, and the diffusion coefficient is extracted from least square fits to Eq.~\ref{msd_longtimes}. A typical result is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:dufresne_results_msd}.

\subsubsection{Blake image system}
In the presence of a boundary, the Green's function of the Stokes equations must be modified to satisfy the no-slip and no-penetration boundary conditions at the solid boundary. This can be achieved using Blake's image construction~\cite{Blake_1971}. A Stokeslet located at $\vect{r}_j$ above the boundary is supplemented by an image system located at $\vect{R}_j=\vect{r}_j - 2h\vect{e}_z$, where $z$ is the height of the colloid centre above the boundary. This image system contains an image Stokeslet, a source doublet and a Stokeslet doublet. Taken together, these singularities cancel the fluid velocity at the boundary and yield the boundary-corrected Green's function. For a single colloid diffusing above a boundary, the leading-order corrections to the diffusion coefficients are:
\begin{align}
    \frac{D_z(z)}{D_0}&=1-\frac{9}{8}\frac{a}{z}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{a^3}{z^3}\right)~,\\
    \frac{D_{xy}(z)}{D_0}&=1-\frac{9}{16}\frac{a}{z}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{a^3}{z^3}\right)~.
    \label{eq:single_particle_boundary_diffusion}
\end{align}
Here, $D_z$ is the diffusion coefficient normal to the boundary, while $D_{xy}$ is the diffusion coefficient parallel to the boundary. A first approximation consists in superimposing the Oseen pair-diffusion correction with the Blake's single colloid-boundary correction. Dufresne \emph{et al.} write this approximation as:
\begin{equation}
    D_\psi^{-1}(r,z)=D_\psi^{-1}(r)+\frac{1}{2}\left[D_{xy}^{-1}(z)-D_0^{-1}\right]~,
    \label{eq:dufresne_linear_superposition}
\end{equation}
where $\psi$ denotes one of the collective or relative pair modes. This linear superposition is shown as dashed lines in Fig.~\ref{fig:dufresne_cross_diffusion}. It captures the qualitative reduction of diffusion near the boundary but fails quantitatively when the colloids are less than approximately $50$ radii away from the plane boundary. Each colloid interacts hydrodynamically with its neighbour, with its own image system, and with the image system of its neighbour. Including these contributions gives the pair diffusion coefficients:
\begin{align}
\frac{D^{C,R}_\perp(r,z)}{D_0}&=1-\frac{9}{16}\frac{a}{z}\pm\frac{3}{4}\frac{a}{r}\left[1-\frac{1+\dfrac{3}{2}\xi}{(1+\xi)^{3/2}}\right]~,\\
\frac{D^{C,R}_\parallel(r,z)}{D_0}&=1-\frac{9}{16}\frac{a}{z}\pm\frac{3}{2}\frac{a}{r}\left[1-\frac{1+\xi+\dfrac{3}{2}\xi^2}{(1+\xi)^{5/2}}
\right]~,
\label{eq:cross_diffusion_modes_dufresne}
\end{align}
where:
\begin{equation}
    \xi=\frac{4z^2}{r^2}~.
\end{equation}
Again, the positive sign corresponds to collective modes, while the negative sign corresponds to relative modes. These expressions are valid up to corrections of orders $\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{a^3}{r^3}\right)$ and $\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{a^3}{z^3}\right)$.

In the far-field limit, where $(r\gg a)$ and $(z\gg a)$, the authors recover the Oseen predictions for two colloids in an unbounded fluid. At smaller elevations, $(z\sim a)$, they capture the leading-order modification of the pair diffusivities by the boundary, and show that confinement weakens the hydrodynamic coupling between the particles. This approximation, however, does not resolve the finite-size lubrication effects that appear in the thin gap limit ($r \sim a$ and $z \sim a$). The hydrodynamic description of this regime is particularly delicate. Directly resolving these thin fluid layers using discrete fluid solvers requires a fine resolution and is computationally demanding either in resources or time. We therefore must find a technique which combines both lubrication corrections to the mobility matrix in the thin-gap regime, and high computational efficiency, in order to reproduce the above results and probe at thinner gaps.


\subsubsection{The RigidMultiblobsWall library and lubrication corrections}
Several numerical approaches have already been developed to account for near-field hydrodynamic interactions. Stokesian dynamics methods combine a multipolar description of the long-range many-body hydrodynamic interactions with explicit pairwise lubrication corrections at short distances \cite{BradyBossis1988,SwanBrady2007}. The fluid is treated as a continuum through Stokes equations, Eq.~\ref{stokes_equations}, and the particle velocities are computed from a mobility problem. Although such methods provide a detailed description of the hydrodynamic interactions, the inclusion of higher moments greatly increases their cost and complexity. Herein, we instead use the minimally resolved, lubrication-corrected Brownian-dynamics method introduced by Delong, Balboa Usabiaga, Donev \emph{et al.}~\cite{Delong2015} and implemented in the \texttt{RigidMultiblobsWall} library.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/stochastichydrotools/rigidmultiblobsboundary}} This method generates Brownian trajectories of dense colloidal suspensions above a no-slip boundary, but does not resolve the surrounding fluid. Long-ranged particle-particle and particle-boundary hydrodynamic interactions are described using the RPY-Blake mobility. At short separations, the RPY-Blake contributions are subtracted and replaced by more accurate sphere-sphere and sphere-boundary near-field asymptotes. The resulting mobility is written schematically as~\cite{sprinkle_driven_2020}:
\begin{equation}
{\tens{\upmu}} =
\left[\tens{\upmu}_{\mathrm{RPB}}^{-1}+\tens{R}_{\mathrm{lub}}^{\mathrm{sup}}-
{\tens{R}}_{\mathrm{RPB}}^{\mathrm{sup}}\right]^{-1},
\end{equation}
where $\tens{\upmu}_{\mathrm{RPB}}$ is the RPY-Blake mobility matrix, $\tens{R}_{\mathrm{lub}}^{\mathrm{sup}}$ is the thin-gap resistance matrix, and ${\tens{R}}_{\mathrm{RPB}}^{\mathrm{sup}}$ is the inaccurate RPY-Blake approximation result in the thin-gap, which must be subtracted (``sup'' indicates the pairwise-superposed near-field resistance matrices). This construction keeps the computational efficiency of a minimally resolved far-field mobility and preserves the lubrication resistance in the thin-gap regime. The lubrication corrections themselves are divided into regimes depending on the width of the gap between the pair and between the pair and the boundary. The library then handles the whole simulation and returns raw positions at each time step. 

\subsection{Results}
It is up to the user to initialise the simulation, and to input all parameters (Table~\ref{tab:physical_parameters}). The time step selection also requires care (as discussed in chapter 1, subsection~\ref{subsec:eulerscheme}). We begin by defining couples of altitude $z$ and pair separation $r$. Each of these couples sets the initial position of the pair of colloids relative to one another and to the boundary. Then, we let the simulation run for a total duration of $N \times t_\mathrm{free} = N \times 83~\mathrm{ms}$, where $N$ corresponds to the number of times we reset the pair positions. We use this trick to replicate Dufresne \emph{et al.}'s trapping protocol, while reducing overheads in the simulation by launching only one very long run with resets instead of $N$ short ones. We then segment the trajectories by reset, and compute collective and relative displacement histograms for each simulation time step. We extract from these histograms the collective and relative MSDs, and verify their exponent: the MSD must grow linearly with time. Finally, we can extract from the MSDs the collective and relative diffusion coefficients (Eq.~\ref{msd_longtimes}). 

As this study was started near the very end of my thesis, the results shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:dufresne_cross_diffusion_vs_simulations} are preliminary. We will in the near future generate trajectories for many more $(h, r)$ pairs, and explore smaller values of the boundary-pair thin gap. However, we already successfully retrieve the experimental results presented in Dufresne \emph{et al.} with a slightly better agreement with the theoretical values from Eqs.~\ref{eq:cross_diffusion_modes_dufresne}. We also explore a smaller pair-boundary gap and smaller pair gaps, and show that the theoretical predictions of Eqs.~\ref{eq:cross_diffusion_modes_dufresne} hold up to a ratio $d/a$ of about 2. Furthermore, we notice a quantitative disagreement for pair separation ratios of about $d/a = 2$ independent of pair-boundary separation, hinting at either a lack of statistics, and/or a need for a more robust theory on the pair-diffusion at small pair gaps.

\subsection{Perspectives}
Using the RBW library, it seems possible to accurately recover the pair-diffusion of colloids with separations well-below the radius range in altitude and pairwise. This could actually shed light on the behaviour of very dense, confined Brownian solutes. To validate this, experiments are currently being carried our in our group. \textcolor{red}{waiting on guirec's explanations for the system.}

The RBW library, however, restricts possible studies in the thin-gap limit to spherical colloids. The lubrication corrections are not implemented for other shapes. This limitation means that we must use other methods for solving for colloids of different sizes or bodies of different geometries. Activity, however, is permitted within the spherical colloid restriction, and we can thus explore the behaviour of active swimmers (see Chapter 5) hydrodynamically interacting in very thin films. 

\section{Take-home message}
This chapter was separated in three main sections. First, we described the motion of a colloid in hydrodynamic interaction with a nearby boundary. We noted the emergence of two modes of motion, mediated by the anisotropy of the effective viscosity felt by the colloid. One mode corresponds to the motion perpendicular to the boundary, whereas the second correspond to the motion in the plane parallel to the boundary. We derived exact and approximate solutions for the expected effective diffusion coefficient and expressed the Langevin equations pertaining to these two modes. We then changed the object of our study to two colloids, diffusing in a bulk fluid. We approximated them as point forces, interacting through a viscous fluid. We derived expressions for their diffusion tensor, as well as expressions for their collective and relative motions along and perpendicular to their line of centre. To account for finite-size effects arising when colloids are under ten radii of separation distance, we mentioned the Rotne-Prager-Yamakawa tensor, and its limitations: it does not account for lubrication divergence at extremely small separations. We coupled pairwise and boundary hydrodynamic contributions through different means. We followed the first hypothesis of Dufresne \emph{et al.}~\cite{Dufresne_2000}, adding pairwise and boundary contributions, which showed to be satisfactory only for very large separations. We then used the Blake image system to derive more accurate expressions for the diffusion tensor. These expressions showed good agreement with the experimental data from Dufresne \emph{et al.}, but poor agreement with numerical simulations using the RigidMultiblobWalls algorithm. We explained this discrepancy as this algorithm uses asymptotical corrections in the pairwise and boundary sub-radius separation regimes. We finally proposed a numerical and experimental co-study to verify these results, which occupy the end of my doctoral research.
