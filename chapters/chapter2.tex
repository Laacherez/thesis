\section{Introduction} 

Let us now turn to the second part of my thesis, and dare I say, the heaviest part of my very own doctoral work, both from the theoretical and the numerical standpoints.

When considering Brownian suspensions, one must remain aware of hydrodynamic interactions at play in the system. Indeed, even at long range, hydrodynamics play a strong role on the behaviour of colloids diffusing in the fluid. Thus, strong care must be taken to ensure that these interactions are accounted for at every range, including the sub-radius range, when a colloid is either close to a confining plane or another colloid. 
Together, we will see that typically, excellent approximations exist for the so-called far-field and moderate-field regimes, but that the near-field regime requires extra care and involves much more complex physics.

\section{Colloid-plane lubrication theory}
Consider now an infinite silica plane, which we will take normal to the vertical $z$ axis, laying at $z=0$. Over that plane is a fluid of density $\rho$, viscosity $\eta$, and thermal energy $k_\mathrm{B} T$ with $k_\mathrm{B}$ Boltzmann's constant. As per usual Stokesian assumptions, we will take the fluid as incompressible.

In that fluid, we now submerge a colloidal silica particle of radius $a$, of the micron scale. The goal is now to accurately simulate such a system in the overdamped regime.
Due to the no-slip boundary conditions at contact with the plane and the colloid's surface, we must modify the effective viscosity of the fluid as the colloid evolves in the $z$ direction. This correction brings not only inhomogeneity along the $z$-axis but also anisotropy, as the $x$ and $y$ components of the friction coefficient will differ from the $z$ component.


In his seminal work\cite{brenner_slow_1961}, Brenner derives the exact expression for the effective viscosity felt by a sphere moving perpendicularly to a plane wall, $\eta_\perp(z)$, as

\begin{equation}
    \label{Brenner}
    \eta_\perp (z) = \eta \frac{4}{3} \sinh(\alpha) \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{n(n+1)}{(2n-1)(2n+3)} \left[ \frac{2 \sinh((2n+1)\alpha) + (2n+1) \sinh(2\alpha)}{4 \sinh^2((n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha) - (2n+1)^2 \sinh^2(\alpha)} - 1 \right]~,
\end{equation}

with $\alpha = \cosh^{-1} \left( \frac{z}{a} \right)$. Because Brenner obtains the effective viscosity from full Stokes derivations, it is worth noting that this expression is exact and valid for all values of $z/a > 1$, that is to say up until colloid/plane contact. However, for the sake of efficient numerical computations later on, we will avoid infinite-series computations and prefer the use of Padé approximants \cite{BakerGravesMorris_1996}, which were computed by Honig \cite{Honig_1971}, Bevan and Prieve \cite{Bevan_2000} as


\begin{equation}
    \label{Padé}
    \eta_\perp (z) \simeq \eta \frac{6z^2 + 2az}{6z^2 - 9az + 2a^2}.
\end{equation}




The parallel effective viscosity, on the other hand, was derived by Fax\'en \cite{Faxen_1924} and is given by the following expression:
\begin{equation}
    \eta_\parallel (z) \simeq  \frac{\eta}{1 - \frac{9}{16} \frac{a}{z} + \frac{1}{8} \left( \frac{a}{z} \right)^3 - \frac{45}{256} \left( \frac{a}{z} \right)^4 - \frac{1}{16} \left( \frac{a}{z} \right)^5}~.
    \label{Faxén}
\end{equation}

These expressions suffice for the simulation of a colloid over an infinite plane in far-field to moderate-field regimes.

\subsection{Summary of potentials in the system}

\subsubsection{Gravitational potential and buoyant mass}




\begin{align}
    U_\mathrm{grav} &  = \Delta m g z~,\\
    & = \frac{4}{3} \pi a^3 (\rho_\mathrm{colloid} - \rho_\mathrm{fluid}) g z~,
    \label{grav_potential}
\end{align}

where $\Delta m$ is the buoyant mass of the colloid, $g$ is the acceleration of gravity, and $\rho_\mathrm{colloid}$ and $\rho_\mathrm{fluid}$ are the mass densities of the colloid and the fluid, respectively.
We may rewrite the above Eq. \ref{grav_potential} as
\begin{equation}
U_\mathrm{grav} = \frac{k_\mathrm{B} T}{l_\mathrm{B}} z~,
\label{grav_potential_lB}
\end{equation}

where $l_\mathrm{B}$ is Boltzmann's length, defined as $l_\mathrm{B} = \frac{k_\mathrm{B} T}{\Delta m g}$. Boltzmann's length quantifies the distance over which gravitational potential energy is comparable to thermal energy.
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsubsection{Electrostatic potential and double-layer interactions}


\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/chapter2/double_layer.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic representation of a colloid of radius $a$ at a distance $z$ from an infinite plane. Both surfaces are charged, and thus, a double layer of counter-ions forms around each of them.}
    \label{double_layer}

\end{figure}
Because of the amphoteric nature of silica, both the infinite place and the colloid surfaces are able to ionise when in contact with water (Fig. \ref{double_layer}). The surfaces themselves become negatively charged, which in turn attract adsorb ions in contact with the surface, in a dense and tightly bound Stern layer. Around this plane, a mobile, ionic "atmosphere" screens the electric potential of the colloid over distance with a characteristic length $l_\mathrm{D}$, for which we provide the derivation below.
This diffuse layer, existing for both surfaces at play in the system, is responsible for the electrostatic repulsion between the two bodies (plane and colloid). 
It is also worth mentioning, for the sake of the following derivation of the electronic interaction potential, that within the diffuse layer exists the so-called Slipping plane. This plane separates the fluid that is dragged by the colloid from the fluid that flows around it. Experimentally speaking, this is where one will measure the electrostatic potential of a surface, the $\zeta$ potential.

At thermodynamic equilibrium, the local ion concentration in the diffuse layer $c_i(\vv{r})$ of a given species should follow the Boltzmann distribution

\begin{equation}
c_i(\vv{r}) = c^\infty_{i} \exp\left\{-\frac{z_i e \psi(\vv{r})}{k_\mathrm{B} T}\right\}~, 
\label{Boltzmann_ion_concentration}
\end{equation}

where $c_{i, \infty}$ is the bulk or infinitely far from surfaces ion concentration, $z_i$ is the valence of the ion, $e$ is the elementary charge, and $\psi(\vv{r})$ is the electrostatic potential at position $\vv{r}$, which satisfies the Poisson equation

\begin{equation}
\nabla^2 \psi(\vv{r}) = - \frac{1}{\varepsilon} \sum_i z_i e c_i(\vv{r})~,
\label{poisson_equation}
\end{equation}

where $\varepsilon$ is the permittivity of the fluid. Substituting the Boltzmann distribution for $c_i(\vv{r})$ into the Poisson equation yields the Poisson-Boltzmann equation, which is a non-linear partial differential equation for $\psi(\vv{r})$.

Thus, combining the above expressions, we get

\begin{equation}
    \nabla^2 \psi(\vv{r}) + \frac{e}{\varepsilon} \sum_i z_i c^\infty_i \exp \left\{\frac{-z_i e \psi(\vv{r})}{k_\mathrm{B}T} \right\} = 0~.
    \label{Psi_full_equation}
\end{equation}

We will only draw our interest to sufficiently dilute solutions in terms of electrolyte concentration $[NaCl]$. In that case, we may Taylor expand the exponential term to obtain 

\begin{equation}
    \nabla^2 \psi(\vv{r}) + \frac{e}{\varepsilon} \sum_i z_i c^\infty_i  \left(1 - \frac{z_i e \psi(\vv{r})}{k_\mathrm{B}T} \right) = 0~.
    \label{Psi_taylor_expanded}
\end{equation}

Since the fluid is electrically neutral
\begin{equation}
    \sum_i z_ic^\infty_i = 0~.
\end{equation}

Thus, Eq. \ref{Psi_taylor_expanded} becomes 

\begin{align}
    \nabla^2 \psi(\vv{r}) &= \left[\sum_i \frac{z_i^2e^2c^\infty_i}{\varepsilon k_\mathrm{B}T}\right] \psi(\vv{r})\\
    & = \frac{1}{l_\mathrm{D}} \psi(\vv{r})~,
    \label{lD_psi}
\end{align}

where $l_\mathrm{D}$ is Debye's length, 

\begin{equation}
l_\mathrm{D} = \sqrt{\sum_i \frac{\varepsilon k_\mathrm{B}T}{z^2_i e^2 c^\infty_i}}~.
\label{debye_length}
\end{equation}

The above expression can be used to compute the Debye length in a given solution. For the case of a monovalent salt in water, we may keep in mind the simple relation $l_\mathrm{D} = 0.304 \sqrt{C}^{-1}$, with C the salt concentration in solution.

Now, we want to compute the electrostatic repulsion potential between a sphere and a plane. To do so, it is convenient to consider the plane as a sphere of infinite radius, thus reducing the problem to a sphere-sphere interaction.
The Debye-Huckel expression for the electrostatic potential around a single charged sphere is given in spherical coordinates by 

\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{r^2}\left[ \frac{\partial}{\partial r} \left( r^2 \frac{\partial \psi(r)}{\partial r}\right) \right] = \frac{1}{l_\mathrm{D}^2}\psi(r)~.
    \label{spherical_debye-huckel}
\end{equation}

This equation has general solution

\begin{equation}
    \psi(r) = K_1 \frac{\exp(\frac{r}{l_\mathrm{D}})}{r} + K_2 \frac{\exp(\frac{-r}{l_\mathrm{D}})}{r}
\end{equation}

Taking into account that $\lim_{r \to \infty}\psi(r) = 0$, we identify $K_1 = 0$. Thus, we can write

\begin{equation}
    \psi(r) = K_2 \frac{\exp(\frac{-r}{l_\mathrm{D}})}{r}~.
\end{equation}

We now are left with the identification of $K_2$. To do so, we use Gauss's flux theorem, and we finally get

\begin{equation}
    \psi(r) = \frac{\sigma a^2}{\varepsilon} \frac{\exp(\frac{a}{l_\mathrm{D}})}{1 + \frac{a}{l_\mathrm{D}}} \frac{\exp\left(\frac{-r}{l_\mathrm{D}}\right)}{r}~,
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ is the charge density at the surface of the sphere, $a$ is its radius. 

Now that we have the electrostatic potential around a single sphere, we can suppose that the potential between two spheres is simply additive by approximation

\begin{equation}
    \psi_\mathrm{tot} (r) = \psi_1(r) + \psi_2(r)~,
\end{equation}

and with $r = a_1 + a_2 + z$ along sphere line of centers and keeping in mind cylindrical symmetry, the total electrostatic interaction potential along the z-direction becomes

\begin{equation}
 U_\mathrm{tot}(z) = \frac{4\pi}{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\sigma_1a_1^2}{1 + l_\mathrm{D}^{-1}a_1}\right)\left(\frac{\sigma_2a_2^2}{1 + l_\mathrm{D}^{-1}a_2}\right) \frac{\exp(-l_\mathrm{D}^{-1}z)}{a_1 + a_2 + z}~.
\end{equation}


We now have the expression of the interaction potential between two charged spheres. As stated above, we may set one of the two radii to infinity and work with this limit to obtain the interaction potential between a sphere and a plane, as

\begin{equation}
\frac{U_\mathrm{elec}}{k_\mathrm{B}T} = B \mathrm{e}^{-\frac{z}{l_\mathrm{D}}}~,
\label{Sphere-Wall interaction}
\end{equation}

where 

\begin{equation}
    B = \frac{4 \pi}{k_\mathrm{B}T \varepsilon}\left( \frac{\sigma a^2}{1 + l_\mathrm{D}^{-1} a}\right) l_\mathrm{D} \sigma_\mathrm{wall}~.
\end{equation}






We will furthermore neglect the attractive Van der Waals interactions, which in our cases of study only play a role within a few nanometers, whereas the repulsive interactions take place up to a few tens of nanometers.





\subsubsection{Equilibrium distribution}
Finally, we may summarize the total potential to which the colloid is subjected when immersed in water above a charged plane as
\begin{equation}
    U(z) = U_\mathrm{g} + U_\mathrm{elec}~,
    \label{total_potentials}
\end{equation}

or, more in detail,

\begin{equation}
    \frac{U(z)}{k_\mathrm{B}T} = B \mathrm{e}^{\frac{-z}{l_\mathrm{D}}} + \frac{z}{l_\mathrm{B}}; ~~~~~~\forall z>0~,
    \label{total_potential_renormalised}
\end{equation}

as we would not want our colloid to cross the boundary.

As I discussed in the Introduction, the probability to find the colloid at position $z$ given its diffusive behaviour, the gravitational and electrostatic potentials, should follow the Gibbs-Boltzmann equilibrium distribution

\begin{equation}
    P_\mathrm{eq} = \frac{1}{Z} \exp \left( - \frac{U(z)}{k_\mathrm{B}T} \right)~.
\end{equation}

This distribution should be normalised, hence the $\frac{1}{Z}$ factor. It is particularly useful to keep the shape of it in mind when designing a simulation, as depending on the effect one wants to probe (very dense suspension, monolayer, or inversely, virtually unbounded colloids in the $z$-plane), we may play with the parameters to get the desired behaviour. \subsubsection{Fokker-Plank equation with multiplicative noise}

Now, in the case of an inhomogeneous and anisotropic diffusion coefficient, the noise in our diffusion equation cannot simply be treated as a Gaussian white noise anymore. Indeed, because its amplitude depends on the diffusivity, and because the diffusivity is neither homogeneous nor isotropic anymore, we will need to include a so-called multiplicative noise in the Langevin equation.
This, as we will see, raises the question of the integration of the multiplicative noise, and the intent of the following derivation is to demonstrate the care that must be taken to ensure we obey detailed balance and do not introduce unphysicality by honest mistake.


We will begin by taking the following Langevin equation (See the Introduction for its formal presentation), but modify the second term in the right-hand side to include a space-dependent amplitude.
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{d}X_t = u(X_t)\mathrm{d}t + g(X_t) \mathrm{d}B_t~,
\label{Fokker-Plank_mulitplicative}
\end{equation}

where, as a reminder, $u(X_t)$ is the drift from external forces acting upon the colloid, $g(X_t)$ is the space-dependent noise amplitude, and $\mathrm{d}B_t$ is a Wiener process, such that $\mathrm{d}B_t = \zeta(s)\mathrm{d}s$, $\langle \mathrm{d}B_t\rangle = 0$ and $\mathrm{d}B_t^2 = \mathrm{d}t$, which can be seen as the integral of a white noise: it is nowhere differentiable.
Integrating Eq. \ref{Fokker-Plank_mulitplicative} over an infinitesimal time step $\Delta t$,

\begin{equation}
    X_{t + \Delta t} = X_t + \int_{t}^{t + \Delta t} u(X_s) \mathrm{d}s + \int_{t}^{t + \Delta t} g(X_s) \mathrm{d}B_s~.
    \label{Fokker-Plank_mulitplicative_discrete}
\end{equation}

It is straightforward to simply integrate the first term of the right-hand side owing to the smoothness of $\mathrm{d}s$ using simple methods such as a forward Euler scheme \cite{burden_numerical_2015}. 
However, the second term of the right-hand side is known as a stochastic integral, where $B_s$ is a process which has infinite variation. In that case, one cannot simply treat $\mathrm{d}B_s$ as a small and deterministic increment, which in turn forbids the use of simple Euler integration.
Thus, in order to compute this second integral, we often express it as 

\begin{equation}
    \int_{t}^{t + \Delta t} g(X_s) \mathrm{d}B_s \equiv g\left([1 - \alpha]X_t + \alpha X_{t+\Delta t}\right)\Delta W(t)~,
    \label{gdBt_equivalence}
\end{equation}

with $\Delta W(t)$ the Wiener increment, as

\begin{equation}
    \Delta W(t) = \int_{t}^{t + \Delta t} \mathrm{d}B_s~,
    \label{Wiener_increment}
\end{equation}


where $\alpha$ encodes where in the time step we want to evaluate the integral. We will need to choose a value for $\alpha$ between $0$ (left endpoint) and $1$ (right endpoint). Of course, conventions for such integrals exist \cite{karatzas_brownian_1991}:

\begin{enumerate}
\item{The Itô convention:} $\alpha = 0$; $\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t} g(X_s)\mathrm{d}B_s \simeq g(X_t)\Delta W$. The system reacts to the noise, there is no feedback within a single timestep. Mathematically, the SDE becomes a \emph{martingale}
\footnote{I was unaware of the reason for using Itô convention over other counterparts prior to writing the manuscript. However, upon reading literature, I understood how practical working with martingales can be. A martingale has a bounded mean, and, given that we know its history up to time $t'< t$, its expected value at $t$ must be $\mathbb{E}\left[M_t | \left\{M_s, s \le t'\right\}\right] = M_{t'}$. In my words, this means that the expected value of a martingale at a given time is the last previously known expected value. 
The name, however, comes from gambling, a field for which I have exactly zero interest to explore.} 
This means that the process's evolution depends strictly on the previous time step, and not on the history before this time step. 
However, no matter how convenient and practical this convention may be, it also entrails a modification, or rather correction to the chain rule of calculus when changing variables. We discuss this further down, as we will be using this convention for our SDE.

\item{The Stratonovich convention:} $\alpha = 1/2$; $\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t} g(X_s)\mathrm{d}B_s \simeq g(\frac{X_t + X_{t + \Delta t}}{2})\Delta W$. Stratonovich proposes a way of circumventing the complexities introduced by Itô's convention in the chain rule, breaking the martignale in the process. We now consider a noise which has a finite, non-zero correlation, but this correlation has a characteristic time that is very small compared to the discretisation time step. I will not detail it as much, as I have not really used this convention and am much less aware of its capabilities and pitfalls.
\end{enumerate}

Mathematically, it is always possible to identify a Stratonovich process to an Itô process with equivalent solutions. Therefore, it is simply a matter of convenience to choose one over the other. There are, of course, subtleties; and I invite the interested reader to read more on the matter in L. Smith's website \cite{oatml_ito_strat_2022} from which I liberally adapted some content within this thesis.
We'll, for the sake of the development, keep $\alpha$ as a variable for the time being.

We begin by taking Eq. \ref{Fokker-Plank_mulitplicative_discrete} to order $\Delta t^{1/2}$ 
\begin{equation}
 X_{t+\Delta t} = X_t + g(X_t) \Delta W(t)
\end{equation}

We can then conveniently develop the $X_{t+\Delta t} - X_t$ term in Eq. \ref{gdBt_equivalence}



\begin{align}
    \int_{t}^{t + \Delta t} g(X_s)\mathrm{d}B_s \,\mathrm{d}s
    &= g\bigl(X_t + \alpha [X_{t + \Delta t}-X_t]\bigr)\,\Delta W(t)\\
    &\approx g\bigl(X_t + \alpha g(X_t)\Delta W(t)\bigr)\,\Delta W(t)\\
    &\approx g(X_t)\Delta W(t) + \alpha g(X_t) g'(X_t)[\Delta W(t)]^2
\end{align}

to order $\Delta t$. This in turn gives

\begin{equation}
X_{t + \Delta t} = X_t + \left[u(X_t) + \alpha g(X_t)g'(X_t)\right] \Delta t + g(X_t) \Delta W(t)~.
\end{equation}

Notice then that the integration of a multiplicative noise brings a correction on the term which depends on $\Delta t$, a.k.a. the \emph{drift} term. 
We want to standardise this expression to easily express the correction to the drift in our equation in order to simulate a trajectory. To do so, we invoke the Fokker-Plank equation yet again
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial P(X_t, t)}{\partial t} = - \frac{\partial}{\partial X_t}K_1(X_t)P(X_t,t) + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial X_t^2}K_2(X_t)P(X_t,t)~,
    \label{FokkerPlank_moments}
\end{equation}


where $K_1$ and $K_2$ respectively are the first and second moments, which we define as

\begin{align}
    K_1(X_t) &= \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta X_t}{\Delta t} \notag\\
             &= u(X_t) + \alpha g(X_t)g'(X_t)
    \label{Fokker_Planck_first_moment}
\end{align}

for the mean, and

\begin{align}
K_2(X_t) & = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{(\Delta X_t)^2}{\Delta t}\\
& = g^2(X_t)
\label{Fokker_plank_second_moment}
\end{align}

for the standard deviation. Thus, we can rewrite Eq. \ref{FokkerPlank_moments} in those new terms:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial P(X_t, t)}{\partial t} = - \frac{\partial}{\partial X_t}\left\{u(X_t) + \alpha g(X_t)g'(X_t)\right\}P(X_t, t) + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial X_t^2}\left\{g(X_t)^2\right\}P(X_t, t)~.
\label{Fokker_plank_final_multiplicative}
\end{equation}



\subsubsection{Derivation of the stochastic drift term}


We would now like to apply this knowledge to compute the new stochastic drift term one must add in the $\mathrm{d}t$ order to achieve physicality in our system. We begin again with the Langevin equation of a colloid sedimenting above a plane

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{d}z = \frac{F(z)}{\gamma_\perp(z)}\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{2D_\perp(z)}\mathrm{d}B_t~.
    \label{confinedLangevin_stochdrift}
\end{equation}


In that case, taking Eq. \ref{Fokker_Planck_first_moment}, we express the mean velocity as
\begin{equation}
\left<\frac{\Delta z}{\Delta t}\right> \equiv \bar{v_d} = \frac{F(z)}{\gamma_\perp(z)} + \alpha \frac{\mathrm{d}D_\perp(z)}{\mathrm{d}z}~.
\label{avg_velocity_stochdrift}
\end{equation}


where $F(z)/\gamma_\perp(z)$ is our term of interest, and it is worth noting it contains the complexities of the following derivations and is, in no way, trivial and simply deriving from potentials. 

We begin by taking the steady-state of Eq. \ref{Fokker_plank_final_multiplicative} and differentiating with respect to time. By definition,

\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z} \left\{-u(z) - \alpha g(z)g'(z) + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z} g(z)^2 \right\} P_\mathrm{steady-state}(z) = 0~.
\end{equation}

with $u(z) = \frac{F}{\gamma_\perp}$ and, because $\lim_{z\to \infty} P_\mathrm{steady~state}(z) = 0$ and $\lim_{z\to \infty} \frac{\mathrm{d} P_\mathrm{steady~state}(z)}{\mathrm{d}z} = 0$,


\begin{equation}
(-u(z) - \alpha g(z)g'(z)) P_\mathrm{steady-state}(z) + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\mathrm{d} }{\mathrm{d} z} \left\{g(z)^2 P_\mathrm{steady-state}(z)\right\} = 0~.
\end{equation}

We may now substitute $A(z) = g^2(z)P_{steady-state}(z)$, which yields

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}A(z)}{\mathrm{d}z} - 2\frac{u(z) + \alpha g(z)g'(z)}{g^2(z)}A(z) = 0~,
\end{equation}

which integrates as

\begin{align}
    \ln \left(A(z)\right) &= \ln \left(g^2(z)\right) + \ln \left(P_\mathrm{steady~state}\right)
    &= \int \frac{u(z') + \alpha g'(z')g(z')}{g^2(z')/2} \mathrm{d}z'~.
\end{align}

And thus,

\begin{equation}
    P_{steady-state}(z) = \frac{1}{g^2(z)} \exp\left\{ 2 \int_0^z \frac{u(z') + \alpha g(z')g'(z')}{g^2(z')} \mathrm{d}z' \right\}~,
\end{equation}

We note that we may express $\ln \left(g^2(z)\right) = \int \frac{2g'(z')g(z')}{g^2(z')}\mathrm{d}z'$, and

\begin{equation}
    U_{steady-state}(z) = -k_\mathrm{B}T \int\frac{u(z') + (\alpha - 1)g(z')g'(z')}{g^2(z')/2} \mathrm{d}z'~.
\end{equation}

Remembering that $F_\mathrm{steady~state} = -\frac{\mathrm{d}U_\mathrm{steady~state}}{\mathrm{d}z}$, we get

\begin{equation}
\frac{F_\mathrm{steady~state}}{\gamma_\perp(z)} = \frac{F(z)}{\gamma_\perp(z)} + (\alpha - 1)g(z)g'(z)~.
\end{equation}

From the Lanvegin equation Eq. \ref{confinedLangevin_stochdrift}, we note that $g(z) = \sqrt{2D_\perp(z)}$, and $F(z) = \frac{u(z)}{\gamma_\perp}$, and we also keep in mind that $D_\perp = \frac{k_\mathrm{B}T}{\gamma_\perp}$. With the Itô convention $\alpha = 0$, we may now write the total drift of Eq. \ref{final_stochastic_drift} as


\begin{equation}
    \bar{v_\mathrm{d}} = \frac{1}{\gamma_\perp(z)} \frac{\mathrm{d}U_\mathrm{steady-state}(z)}{\mathrm{d}z} + \frac{\mathrm{d}D_\perp(z)}{\mathrm{d}z}~.
\end{equation}


By identification, we then recover the deterministic drift velocity emerging from external forces
\begin{equation}
v_\mathrm{d} = \frac{k_\mathrm{B}T}{\gamma_\perp(z)} \left[-\frac{1}{l_\mathrm{D}}B \exp \left(-\frac{z}{l_\mathrm{D}}\right) + \frac{1}{l_\mathrm{B}} \right]~,
\end{equation}

whereas the stochastic drift, in the Padé approximation, reads

\begin{equation}
v_\mathrm{stochastic~drift} = 2D_0a\frac{2a^2 + 12 az + 21 z^2}{(2a^2 + 9az + z^2)^2}~.
\label{final_stochastic_drift}
\end{equation}

In the latter parts of this chapter, I will use an algorithm that is specifically made to handle Brownian motion in confined geometries. It is worth keeping in mind the present derivation holds for this algorithm, which is why I deemed it relevant to be included in this thesis.

\subsubsection{Simple Langevin simulation of a confined colloid}

A very usual task in my group, thanks to the theses produced and defended by Maxime Lavaud \cite{maxime_thesis}and Elodie Millan \cite{elodie_thesis}, is to simulate the system I just spent a few pages describing throughly. \subsection{Coupling colloid-wall and colloid-colloid lubrication theories}
Naturally, the next step is to couple both lubrication effects together. Luckily, such study has already been carried out both theoretically and experimentally by Dufresne et al. \cite{Dufresne_2000}. In this article, the authors discuss the modification on colloid cross-diffusion due to wall and colloid-colloid-induced lubrication effects.
The following setup, depicted in Figure \ref{dufresne_system}, is used: two colloids of radius $a=0.485\pm0.025 \mu \mathrm{m}$ (Duke Scientific lot 21024) are submerged in a water-salt solution of 2mM [NaCl] and viscosity $\eta = 0.817 \mathrm{cP}$. 
The water-salt layer is $140 \pm 2 \mu \mathrm{m}$ thick, and sealed between a microscope slide and a No. 1 coverslip, with the system fully sealed with UV-cured epoxy to prevent evaporation of the fluid and fluid flows in the bulk. The system is maintained at $T = 29.00 \pm 0.01 ^\circ \mathrm{C}$. In these conditions, the colloid self-diffusion is found to be $D_0 = \frac{k_\mathrm{B}T}{6 \pi \eta a} = 0.550 \pm 0.028 \mu \mathrm{m}^2/\mathrm{s}$.

    \label{dufresne_system}
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/chapter2/dufresne_system.pdf}
    \caption{Experimental setup of Dufresne et al. \cite{Dufresne_2000} for the study of two colloids diffusing over a plane wall.}

The authors then trap the two colloids with a single optical tweezer, by alternating the position of the focal plane at the two positions of interest at a frequency of 200Hz using a galvanometer-driven mirror. Then, by blocking the beam for $t=83\mathrm{ms}$, they retrieve the sphere positions over five video frames only to ensure minimal out-of-focal displacement.
From there on, authors carry out experiments over a grid of starting positions in $(r, h)$ space, with $r$ the center-to-center distance between colloids and $2 \mu \mathrm{m}< r < 10 \mu \mathrm{m}$, and $h$ the colloid center-to-wall distance, with $1\mu \mathrm{m}< h < 30 \mu \mathrm{m}$.
 By reactivating the trapping, they can repeat the experiment with perfect reproducibility for each starting position of interest in their system.


As discussed in the previous sections, colloids in a same bath should see their motion coupled through hydrodynamic interactions, and the coupling to the wall itself should also modify their expected diffusion. To investigate these coupling effects, the authors decompose the trajectories of the colloids into cooperative and relative motions, respectively $\vv\rho = \vv{r_1} + \vv{r_2}$ and $\vv{r} = \vv{r_1} - \vv{r_2}$ along ($\parallel$) and perpendicular ($\perp$) directions to the line of centers of the colloids. From there, they fill displacement histograms for each mode of motion at each of the five time steps taken into consideration and retrieve the effective diffusion coefficients from the standard deviation of each histogram, as

\begin{equation}
    \langle \Delta r_{i, \alpha} ( \tau ) \Delta r_{j, \beta} \rangle = 2 D_{i\alpha, j\beta} \tau~,
\end{equation}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

with $i,j \in \{1,2\}$ and $\alpha, \beta \in \{\parallel, \perp\}$, where $\tau$ is the elapsed time, and where $\langle . \rangle$ denotes an ensemble average.

A typical result of the experiment for a given set of $(r, h)$ is shown in Figure \ref{dufresne_results_msd}. There, MSDs are extracted from displacement histograms at each of the five time lags into consideration. From there, authors can extract the effective cross-diffusion coefficient.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \label{dufresne_results_msd}
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/chapter2/dufresne_results.pdf}
    \caption{Adapted from \cite{Dufresne_2000}. Measured pair diffusion for the relative perpendicular mode of motion of the two colloids. The upper inset shows the histogram of all binned displacements for a given time step, for $h = 25.5 \pm 0.7 \mu \mathrm{m}$ and $r = 7.00 \pm 0.25 \mu \mathrm{m}$. For comparisons purposes with their experimentally retrieved cross-diffusions in the four possible modes, the authors then compute by different means the theoretical expected value of the cross-diffusion coefficients.
In the case of two colloids in the bulk that are separated by a sufficient distance to approximate them by point forces, the flow field around one of the colloids can be described by the fundamental solution to Stokes' equations for a point force, the Stokeslet \cite{Oseen_1927}, and the Green's function for the flow at $\vv{x}$ in the $\alpha$ direction from a unit force at $\vv{r}_j$ in the $\beta$ direction reads :

\begin{equation}
    G^S_{\alpha \beta} (\vv{x} - \vv{r}_j) = \frac{1}{8 \pi \eta} \left( \frac{\delta_{\alpha \beta}}{|\vv{x} - \vv{r_j}|} + \frac{(\vv{x} - \vv{r_{j}})_\alpha(\vv{x} - \vv{r_{j}})_\beta}{|\vv{x} - \vv{r}_j|^3} \right).
\end{equation}

Then, for the case of two colloids of same radius, they recover the following expression for the cross-diffusion coefficient in the bulk:

\begin{align}
 \frac{D^{C,R}_\perp}{2D_0}& = 1 \pm \frac{3}{4} \frac{a}{r} + \mathcal{O} \left( \frac{a^3}{r^3} \right)~,\\
 \frac{D^{C,R}_\parallel}{2D_0} &= 1 \pm \frac{3}{2} \frac{a}{r} + \mathcal{O} \left( \frac{a^3}{r^3} \right)~,
 \label{Oseen}
\end{align}

where positive and negative corrections correspond respectively to the collective and relative modes of motion. These expressions are known as the Oseen approximations \cite{Oseen_1927}, and are valid solely in the far-field regime due to the point-force approximation not taking into account the finite-size effects.

Now, in the presence of a wall, one must take extra care to ensure no-slip boundary conditions at the wall as well as no fluid penetration through the wall itself. To do this, one may use the single-colloid method of images \cite{Blake_1971}, where a Stokeslet (S), as Source Doublet (D) and a Stokeslet Doublet (SD) of opposite strength to the Stokeslet of interest at $\vv{r_j}$ above the wall is placed at equal distance below the wall, at $\vv{R_j} = \vv{r_j} - 2h \vv{e_z}$. The goal of the mirror Stokeslet is to cancel most of the normal penetration at the wall. The Source Doublet ensures no-slip at the wall, and the Stokeslet Doublet cancels remaining penetration. Then, the flow at the position of the image colloid is written

\begin{equation}
    \label{image_system}
    G^W_{\alpha\beta} \left(\vv{x} - \vv{R_j}\right) = - G^S_{\alpha\beta}\left(\vv{x} - \vv{R_j}\right) + 2 h ^2 G^D_{\alpha\beta}\left(\vv{x} - \vv{R_j}\right) - 2 h G^{SD}_{\alpha\beta}\left(\vv{x} - \vv{R_j}\right)~,
\end{equation}

with 

\begin{align}
    \label{Image_system_details}
    G^D_{\alpha\beta} (\vv{x} - \vv{R_j}) &= \frac{1}{8 \pi \eta} \left( 1 - 2 \delta_{\beta z} \right) \frac{\partial}{\partial R_{j, \beta}} \left( \frac{(\vv{x} - \vv{R_j})_\alpha}{|\vv{x} - \vv{R_j}|^3} \right)~,\\
    G^{SD}_{\alpha\beta} (\vv{x} - \vv{R_j}) &= (1 - 2\delta_{\beta z}) \frac{\partial}{\partial R_{j, \beta}} G^S_{\alpha z} (\vv{x} - \vv{R_j})~.
\end{align}

which yields the two following modes of motion for the cross-diffusion coefficients

\begin{align}
    \label{cross_diffusion_modes}
    \frac{D_z}{D_0} &=   1 - \frac{9}{8} \frac{a}{r}  + \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{a^3}{r^3}\right)~,\\
    \frac{D_{xy}}{D_0} &=   1 - \frac{9}{16} \frac{a}{r} + \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{a^3}{r^3}\right)~.
\end{align}

One may notice the resemblance between the correction terms in the above expressions and the corrections in the Faxén formula from equation \ref{Faxén}, which is not surprising as both corrections are due to the same wall-induced hydrodynamic effects. However, these expressions are only valid in the far-field regime, and fail to capture the lubrication effects in the near-field regime, which is precisely the regime of interest for our simulations.

The authors then propose an intermediate regime, first by naively superimposing the Oseen and Blake approximations as $D_\psi^{-1}(r, h) = D_\psi^{-1}(r) + \left[D_{xy}^{-1}(h) - D_0^{-1}\right]/2$ (in dash lines in Figure \ref{Dufresne_cross_diffusion}), which is a crude approximation which fails to capture the behaviour of the cross-diffusion for colloids below 50 radii from the plane. The authors then propose that the colloid must interact with its image, its neighbour, and its neighbour's image. In that case, the cross-diffusion is given by

\begin{align}
\frac{ D^{C,R}_\perp }{ 2D_0 } &= 1 - \frac{9}{16} \frac{a}{r} \pm \frac{3}{4} \frac{a}{r} \left( 1 - \frac{ 1 + \frac{3}{2}\xi }{ (1 + \xi )^{3/2} } \right) \\
\frac{ D^{C,R}_\parallel }{ 2D_0 } &= 1 - \frac{9}{16} \frac{a}{r} \pm \frac{3}{2} \frac{a}{r} \left( 1 - \frac{1 + \xi + \frac{3}{2} \xi^2}{ (1 + \xi )^{5/2} }\right)
\label{cross_diffusion_modes_dufresne}
\end{align}

with $\xi = 4h^2 / r^2$ and up to $\mathcal{O} \left( \frac{a^3}{r^3}\right)$ and $\mathcal{O} \left( \frac{a^3}{h^3}\right)$ (in solid lines in Figure \ref{Dufresne_cross_diffusion}).
